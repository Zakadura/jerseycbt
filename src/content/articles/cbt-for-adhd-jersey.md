*Practical support for the gap between knowing what to do and being able to do it.*

The email needs a two-minute reply. You open it, notice another message, remember something urgent and switch tasks. By evening, the reply is still unwritten. Now there is another problem: the familiar thought, “Why can’t I manage something so simple?”

For some adults with ADHD, ordinary demands can take a disproportionate amount of effort. If you are looking for **CBT for ADHD in Jersey, Channel Islands**, it helps to understand what therapy can offer and where it fits alongside medication and practical adjustments.

## What NICE recommends for adults with ADHD

NICE, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, sets out its ADHD treatment recommendations in guideline NG87. For adults, medication has a central role, while psychological support is considered according to the person’s needs and preferences.

The recommendations distinguish three situations:

- **Medication:** offer it when ADHD continues to cause significant impairment in at least one area of life after environmental modifications have been introduced and reviewed.
- **Psychological treatment without medication:** consider it when someone makes an informed choice not to take medication, has difficulty taking it consistently, or finds it ineffective or intolerable.
- **Psychological treatment alongside medication:** consider it when medication has helped but significant difficulties remain.

Where psychological treatment is indicated, NICE specifies structured support focused on ADHD, with regular follow-up in person or by phone. It says treatment “may involve elements of or a full course of CBT”. A full CBT course is therefore an option, rather than a requirement for every adult. [NICE NG87, recommendations 1.5.15–1.5.18](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations#managing-adhd)

### Making the environment easier to work in

Environmental modifications are changes that reduce the impact of ADHD: fewer interruptions, a quieter workspace, written instructions or shorter periods of concentration with movement breaks. The useful adjustment depends on the person and the task. [NICE’s explanation of environmental modifications](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations#terms-used-in-this-guideline)

This recommendation recognises that surroundings matter. It should not be read as an instruction to struggle alone before asking for help. Assessment, information and shared treatment planning remain part of care.

## How CBT for ADHD can help in daily life

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition. CBT aims to build usable skills and address patterns that add to the difficulty. It looks at how thoughts, feelings and actions influence one another, while taking account of difficulties with attention, organisation and following through on intentions.

An established CBT approach for adults combines planning and organisation, managing distractions and developing more balanced thinking. It can also address procrastination. Practical work might include:

- **Keeping track of commitments:** finding one manageable place for appointments, deadlines and tasks.
- **Starting a task:** breaking “finish the report” into a first action that can actually be attempted.
- **Managing distractions:** noticing what pulls attention away and rehearsing a way to return.
- **Planning realistically:** checking time estimates against experience and allowing for interruptions.
- **Responding to setbacks:** examining harsh conclusions such as “I always fail” and finding a more accurate response.

These are examples of work within ADHD-focused CBT, adapted to the individual rather than a standard list everyone must complete. [Description of CBT for adults with ADHD, Sprich and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3874265/)

Consider an illustrative example: someone keeps postponing a report because they expect to get it wrong. Avoiding it brings short-term relief, but the approaching deadline increases anxiety.

A therapist and client might agree on a small experiment: open the document, write three rough points and work for ten minutes with notifications silenced. Afterwards, they examine what happened. Was the first step clear enough? Did the timer help? Did fear of making a mistake still get in the way?

The task and the emotional response can both become part of the work. A missed deadline may need a better reminder system; the belief that it proves personal worthlessness needs a different kind of attention.

### Making therapy manageable

Therapy itself asks for planning, remembering and practice. Sessions should make room for those difficulties. A clear agenda, brief written reminders, practising a strategy together and agreeing one realistic step between appointments may make the work easier to use.

If a strategy repeatedly falls apart, that is useful information. It may need simplifying, a different cue or a change in the environment. The review matters as much as the original plan.

## What the research tells us

In a randomised trial involving 86 adults who still had ADHD symptoms while taking medication, ADHD-focused CBT produced greater symptom improvement than relaxation with educational support. This supports CBT as a possible addition to medication for persistent symptoms. It does not establish that therapy replaces medication or that everyone will benefit equally. [Safren and colleagues, JAMA, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20736471/)

Useful therapy goals should also be personal and observable: beginning important work earlier, keeping appointments more reliably or recovering from a setback with less self-criticism. Progress can be reviewed against those goals.

## How the guidance differs for children and young people

NICE recommends ADHD-focused group parent training first for children under five. Medication requires a second specialist opinion from a service experienced in this age group.

For children aged five and over, parents and carers should receive ADHD information and additional support, which can be group based. Medication is offered when significant impairment persists after environmental changes, following discussion and a baseline assessment.

For young people who benefit from medication but remain significantly impaired, NICE says to consider CBT covering peer relationships, problem-solving, self-control, listening and understanding and expressing feelings. Adult treatment advice should not simply be applied to children. [NICE NG87, recommendations 1.5.7–1.5.14](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87/chapter/recommendations#managing-adhd)

## Finding ADHD support in Jersey

If you suspect ADHD and want an assessment, speak to your GP. The Government of Jersey describes referral through a GP or Autism Jersey to its adult ADHD and Autism Diagnostic Service. Its website also lists ADHD Jersey as a source of information and support. [Jersey’s adult assessment and support information](https://www.gov.je/Health/Mental/AdultMentalHealth/MentalHealthServices/pages/adhdautism.aspx)

NICE provides the treatment framework discussed here; local referral arrangements and available services need to be checked separately.

For psychological support, an initial conversation can explore the difficulties you want help with, any existing diagnosis or treatment, and whether CBT is appropriate. Decisions about medication belong with your prescriber.
